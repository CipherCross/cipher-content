##       **Business / Professional Content Questions** 

  

1.  What’s a recent project or initiative you’ve led that taught you something unexpected?  
    That you can say no to toxic clients. It’s a hard decision to deliberately choose to lose revenue. But it’s also a mission of a CEO to identify bad relationships that hurt your business and stop them to minimize damage. We stopped working with a client after 6 months because the relationship was hurting us and them. It was a gentle stop, where we handled transition effectively: documentation, support and onboarding of a new team.  
      
2.  How do you ensure your work creates a meaningful, positive impact for users?  
    For me, it boils down to two core principles: intention and proximity. First, intention. Everything we build has to start from the question, "Is this genuinely making someone's life easier or more enjoyable?" Second, and I think more importantly, is proximity. It's about closing the distance between the people building the product and the people using it. My partner, Volodymyr, is brilliant at seeing the macro view in the data, but I make it my job to stay close to the ground truth. It’s actually a non-negotiable part of my Monday morning routine to read through all the new user feedback and support tickets from the previous week.  
      
      
3.  What’s a challenge in wellness or mental health that you feel is often overlooked?  
    User experience. Applications become more and more competitive trying to solve every possible problem user might have. What if we created a laser-focused application to solve 1 problem and appreciate user for doing so? I think this is what is currently needed on the market.  
      
4.  How do you balance empathy for users with business goals in your role?  
    I see it as a cause-and-effect relationship, not a balancing act.

Empathy is the input; a healthy business is the output. In the long term, our business goals are only achievable if our users feel understood and valued. A positive user experience is the most sustainable foundation for growth.  
  

1.  What’s one innovative approach your team has tried recently that showed promising results?  
    Using AI for business process documentation. This automated 80% of repetitive tasks for our business analyst. Documenting the valid and invalid cases, and user flows. When the most important part is always the core logic which is documented manually.  
      
2.  How has your role evolved in the past year, and what skills helped you adapt?  
    Self-doubt :) That is a toxic trait, but if you are aware of it, you can use it as your own weapon. What if there is a better approach? What if you made a mistake? double-checking is always a good practice that allows you to further polish your solution. Just apply it with caution and don’t over doubt yourself :)    
      
3.  What do you wish more people understood about your work in wellness/mental health?  
    That it’s a marathon, not a sprint. We live in a culture that wants instant results, but genuine mental health doesn’t work that way. It’s not a single therapy session or one retreat that 'fixes' you.

It's the slow, consistent work of building a foundation. It's about designing a personal system of habits—like knowing when you need a 3-hour hike versus 30 minutes of reels—that supports you day-in and day-out. It’s a lifelong practice of self-awareness, not a problem to be solved and then forgotten.  
  

1.  Can you share a story where a small action in your work led to a big user impact?  
    Of course. Here is a story that reflects the established personality.

This happened a few months ago. We run a platform that helps small, local businesses—think artisans, tour guides, small workshop hosts—manage their bookings. It’s part of the sustainable business my partner and I are building.  
We kept noticing a pattern in our support tickets. A significant number of them were from frustrated vendors, especially those new to the platform, complaining about getting bookings at completely wrong times. A pottery class in Lisbon would get a booking for 2 AM, for example. It was creating chaos for them and a lot of negative first impressions. My first thought was that we needed a big, complex tutorial or a redesign of the whole scheduling page. But before diving into a huge project, I decided to just sit down and replicate the exact sign-up process myself, as if I were a new user. The culprit was a tiny detail. When a vendor set up their business hours, the time zone field defaulted to UTC (Coordinated Universal Time). Now, to a developer, that makes sense as a universal standard. But to a baker in Alfama setting her hours, it's meaningless. She’d input "9:00 AM," assuming it was Lisbon time, but the system would register it as 9:00 AM in the UTC time zone, which is an hour earlier here in Lisbon. During the summer, that could be even more off. The fix was incredibly small. We wrote a simple script that automatically detected the user's browser time zone and set that as the default in the dropdown menu. The user could still change it, but for 99% of them, the correct one was now pre-selected. It took one of our developers maybe 30 minutes to implement. The impact was immediate and massive. Within a month, support tickets related to scheduling errors dropped by over 90%. But the real win wasn't just the reduction in complaints. It was the shift in user experience. The system suddenly felt smarter, more intuitive. We stopped being a "confusing platform" and became a tool that "just works." That small act of meeting the user where they were, instead of forcing them to understand our logic, rebuilt trust and removed a huge point of friction. It was a powerful lesson: sometimes the biggest impact comes not from adding a new feature, but from finding and removing one small, invisible obstacle.  
  

1.  How do you stay motivated and inspired by your work when progress feels slow?  
    This is an extremely hard situation. My life taught me a good lesson: you must stay believing in your success. If you don’t work hard, you can’t expect results. Because there will be a question: have I done everything in my power to succeed? if the answer is no, you won’t rest. This is why I remain persistent throughout the slow progress periods. Success comes in waves, so you can expect a relatively stall sea :)   
      
2.  What upcoming trend, tool, or idea in wellness & mental health are you curious to explore?  
    I'm genuinely curious about the new wave of wearable technology focused specifically on biometric data for mental health.  
      
    I imagine being able to directly correlate my choices with their impact. For instance, I know subjectively that a long hike recharges me, but I'd love to actually see the quantified proof in my recovery score the next day. It would be a powerful feedback loop. I could compare the data from a day with a long hike versus a day stuck in meetings and see the physiological proof of what I already feel.

# Post 1  

My first startup didn’t change the world.

  

It didn’t even survive its second year.

  

Yet looking back, that chapter taught me more than any success story could.

We started with big dreams and amazing designs.

  
What we didn’t have - beyond optimism - was a clear revenue model or a realistic timeline.

  

We thought a great app would sell itself.  
Spoiler: it doesn’t.

  

Here’s my honest carousel of Do’s & Don’ts from the firsthand:

  

DO’s:  
→ Validate the revenue plan before the design deck:  
Don’t just build a product - build a business.  
Revenue thinking can’t wait until launch.

  

→ Focus on timing:  
Sometimes, an early idea is just... too early.  
Study the market cycles and user readiness before you leap.

  

→ Talk to real users, not just peers:  
Actual feedback from people willing to pay is more valuable  
than ten opinions from friends.

  

DON'Ts:  
→ Don’t leave monetization as an “add-on”:  
If you can’t explain how you’ll make money on a whiteboard,  
you can’t explain it to investors or customers.

  

→ Don’t confuse interest for commitment:  
“I love the idea\!” isn’t the same as, “Here’s my card.”  
Validate with real actions, not just words.

  

→ Don’t ignore the “boring” stuff:  
Legal, support, maintenance, and user onboarding are  
what make dreams sustainable.

  

What would I wish to know before the start?

Failure, handled with honesty, is the best accelerator.  
Our team became better builders, better listeners, and (eventually\!) better entrepreneurs.

  

Every future app or launch starts with one question:  
“What problem are we solving, and how will it sustain itself?”

  

If you’re building something now, or thinking about that first move - remember, you don’t need to fail to learn.

  
But if you do, make every lesson count.

# Autumn Post 6  

A few months ago, our support team started noticing a recurring issue on our platform that helps small, local businesses manage their bookings:

Vendors were getting bookings at completely wrong times. A pottery class in Lisbon could receive a booking for 2 AM. A small workshop host might get a customer arriving at the wrong time.   
For vendors who are just starting on our platform, it was confusing, frustrating, and created negative first impressions. Support tickets were piling up, and I realized this small problem was affecting trust in our system.

My first instinct was to redesign the entire scheduling system.   
I imagined a big project: new tutorials, redesigning pages, maybe even rethinking the architecture. But before diving into something complex, I decided to approach it differently - I went through the sign-up process myself, exactly as a new user would.

And then I found it.

The culprit was a tiny detail: the time zone field defaulted to UTC. To a developer, that’s logical - a universal standard. But for a local vendor in Lisbon, it made no sense. They would enter "9:00 AM" assuming it was local time, but the system registered it as 9:00 AM UTC. During daylight saving periods, the timing could be even more off.

So the solution?   
Small, simple, and elegant.   
We wrote a script to detect the user’s browser time zone automatically and pre-select it in the dropdown. Users could still change it if needed, but for 99% of them, it just worked. One developer implemented it in about 30 minutes.

The impact was immediate:

1\) Support tickets related to scheduling errors dropped by over 90%.  
2\) Vendors felt that the platform finally “just worked.”  
3\) Trust and user confidence increased dramatically.  
  

This experience reminded me of a powerful lesson: sometimes the biggest impact comes not from adding a new feature, but from removing a tiny obstacle that your users don’t even realize is there.

It also reinforced our company philosophy at CipherCross: always meet users where they are.   
Build systems that feel intuitive, that reduce friction, and that focus on the human experience behind every interaction.

Small details matter. They build trust, reduce frustration, and can transform user experience - sometimes more than any flashy feature ever could.
